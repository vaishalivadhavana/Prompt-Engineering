A hands-on guide for Namibian law enforcement officials: how large language models work, where they fail, and how to write prompts that make ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Perplexity useful partners in cybercrime and digital-evidence work, without giving them real case data.

What is an LLM?

A Large Language Model is a program trained on huge amounts of text (books, websites, reports, code) that learned the statistical patterns of language. Given some text, it predicts the most likely next piece of text. It does not look facts up or reason like a detective.

Tokens

Text is cut into tokens (about ¾ of an English word each). Your prompt, any files and the reply must all fit inside the model's context window. Share a sample of a large log file, not the whole thing.

Prediction, not verification

The model writes the most plausible-sounding continuation. A made-up IP address owner sounds just as plausible as a real one.

Numbers are awkward

Amounts like 2,150.75 get split into pieces, so mental arithmetic slips. For totals and counts, tell the tool to run code.

Four limitations every investigator must know

1. Hallucination

Invented sections of an Act, fake case citations, a confident but made-up "owner" of a phone number. Rule: verify every legal reference in the official statute or Government Gazette.

2. Knowledge cutoff

New scam types, new app versions and recent amendments may be missing. Rule: use a search-enabled tool and open the cited source yourself.

3. Bias

Assuming a foreign context, or stereotyping a community, region or language group. Rule: ask "what else could explain this?" and never let AI output name a suspect.

4. No human judgment

It cannot decide reasonable suspicion, admissibility, chain of custody or whether to arrest. Rule: those calls belong to you and the prosecutor.

The golden rule: AI assists, the officer decides

Good uses

Explain a technical concept · summarise a long public report · draft a timeline from mock data · suggest what to check next · critique your report as a defence lawyer would · quiz you before a court appearance.

Must stay with you

The decision to investigate · what counts as evidence · chain of custody · identifying suspects · legal grounds for any action · the signed statement and final report, in your own words.
Never paste into a public AI tool: real names, ID or phone numbers, victim or witness statements, case numbers, informant details, unreleased evidence, or anything classified. Use fictional or fully anonymised data. Follow your agency's AI policy, and check each tool's privacy settings (for example training opt-out or temporary chat) before use.

Ground it. Give the tool the document or data and tell it to answer only from that. Grounded answers hallucinate far less.

Four types of prompting

1. Zero-shot: just ask

These server log times are in UTC. Convert each to Namibian time (CAT, UTC+2), keep the order, and state the assumption you made:
2026-03-02 07:14, 2026-03-02 07:20, 2026-03-02 07:31, 2026-03-02 08:05

2. Few-shot: show examples first

Classify each scam message as Phishing, Investment, Romance or Other.

"Your wallet is locked. Click this link to verify" → Phishing
"Double your N$ in 24 hours, send to this number" → Investment
"I am a soldier abroad and need your help to send money" → Romance

Now classify:
"Your parcel is held at customs, pay N$150 to release it"
"Join our group chat for guaranteed forex returns"
"I love you, please buy me a gift card so I can visit"

3. Chain-of-thought: reason in steps

Six victims each sent money to one wallet, which then forwarded it onward. Before recommending anything, work step by step:
1. What pattern do these transfers show?
2. What are two innocent explanations?
3. What extra data would separate the explanations?
4. Which lawful channel would I use to get that data?
5. Your final assessment, with one line of reasoning. Do not name any person.

Advanced techniques

Structured output. Instead of "tell me about tools", ask: "Compare Autopsy, Wireshark and Cellebrite-style extraction tools in a table: Best for | Skill needed | Cost type | Common pitfall."

Allow "I don't know"

If the data doesn't support a conclusion, or you need information you don't have, say so instead of guessing. Mark anything uncertain with [CHECK].

Ask me first

I need a plan to trace the flow of money in a mobile-wallet scam (mock case). Before suggesting anything, ask me up to 5 questions about my data, powers and limits that would change your advice.

Prompt chaining

  1. "Describe this dataset and list its data quality problems."
  2. "Write code to fix problems 1 and 3. Leave 2; I will decide."
  3. "Now build a timeline of the cleaned data."
  4. "Draft a methods note describing exactly what we did, so it can be explained in court."

Self-critique

Now review your own analysis as a sceptical prosecutor would. What did you assume? What could be wrong? What would you verify before relying on this?

Checklist and next steps

  • An LLM predicts the next token. Plausible does not mean correct.
  • Write prompts with role, task, context, instructions and output format.
  • Verify every number, citation and legal reference; ask the tool to run code for calculations.
  • Keep real case, victim and informant data out of public tools.
  • Run the same prompt in more than one tool and compare.
  • AI assists. The officer decides and signs.

Reusable project instructions

You are my training assistant for cybercrime investigation practice. Use only the mock files I provide. Run code for every calculation; never estimate numbers. Never name or accuse a real person. If unsure or missing information, say so and mark [CHECK]. Never invent legal sections or case citations. When I ask for feedback on my writing, give comments instead of rewriting it.
